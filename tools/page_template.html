A macropad with a screen: three printable starting points

Each concept is a tilted 3D-printed case with Cherry MX hot-swap keys, an EC11 knob and a small SPI LCD, run by an nRF52840 with the same Zephyr firmware and config app as the trackball. All three come from one parametric OpenSCAD file, and every part fits a 180 mm printer bed.

    How the inside works

    The same rules apply to all three concepts. Every number here is a parameter at the top of macropad.scad.

    {{STACK}}

    The lid is the switch plate

    MX switches clip into a 14 mm square hole in a 1.5 mm plate. The top skin is 3 mm thick: the first 1.5 mm has the 14 mm hole, and below it a 15 mm pocket gives the clips room to spring open. The 1.5 mm left between neighbouring pockets acts as a grid of ribs, which keeps a printed plate from flexing when you press a key.

    Hot-swap PCB 5 mm below the plate

    The MX standard puts the PCB top 5.0 mm under the plate top. Kailh hot-swap sockets sit under the PCB (1.85 mm), so switches push in and pull out without soldering. The PCB hangs from the switches and is pulled up against small pillars between the keys with M2 screws, so it can't sag when you pull a keycap.

    The tilt makes room for the battery

    The top is tilted 6–7°, but the bottom plate is flat. The space under the PCB therefore grows toward the back: 2 mm under the front sockets, and 10 mm or more under the screen. The LiPo, controller and display all sit in that rear band, so the front stays a low 12.5 mm.

    Screen behind a 1 mm bezel

    The window is the active pixel area plus 0.4 mm, with a 45° chamfer so the bezel doesn't shade the edge pixels when you look at the screen at an angle. The glass sits in a pocket from below and a printed clamp holds the module board with two M2 screws. The main PCB has a cut-out there, and the module connects with 8 short wires (SPI, power and backlight).

    Controller face down at the back

    A Pro Micro-size nRF52840 board hangs under the PCB on 2.5 mm headers, with its USB-C facing the rear wall. The wall opening is 13 × 7.5 mm, sized for the cable's moulded plug rather than the metal plug. A hole that only fits the metal part is a common printed-case mistake: the plug never seats fully.

    Four screws and heat-set inserts

    Four M3 countersunk screws go up through the bottom plate into short M3 brass inserts, which you press into the corner bosses with the soldering iron (around 220 °C for PLA). A 1.5 mm lip on the bottom plate locates it inside the walls, a cradle holds the LiPo, and four 10 mm rubber feet sit in shallow recesses. A slide switch on the right wall cuts the battery.

    Electronics and firmware

    Worked out for all three concepts while the choice is open. The firmware compiles; nothing has run on hardware yet.

    Controller: Pro Micro-size nRF52840

    Chosen: the "SuperMini nRF52840" (about £4.50), a clone of the nice!nano v2 (about £25, a drop-in swap if a SuperMini disappoints). The same chip as your DK and trackball, 18 GPIO on through-hole pins, a LiPo charger and USB-C. Zephyr's board target is promicro_nrf52840/nrf52840/uf2. It ships with a UF2 bootloader: new firmware is a file you drag onto a USB drive.

    Two things in its pin choice matter. Nordic marks ten of its pins "low frequency only", so the LCD clock and data use two full-speed pins and the slow signals (keys, knob, reset, backlight) take the restricted ones. And on the SuperMini the 3.3 V pin that powers the LCD stays off until firmware drives P0.13 high. Some SuperMini batches have a faulty 32 kHz crystal, so the firmware uses the chip's own RC clock for Bluetooth instead.

    Firmware: firmware/

    • Keys: gpio-kbd-matrix plus input-keymap, one 1N4148W per switch, knob push in the matrix.
    • Knob: gpio-qdec, which sleeps on a GPIO interrupt between turns. Volume, push to mute.
    • Screen: sitronix,st7789v or galaxycore,gc9x01x with LVGL 9. At 16 MHz a full 320 × 240 frame takes about 80 ms, one tile about 6 ms. Tiles light up while their key is held; backlight off after 60 s.
    • HID: keyboard and media reports over USB (same sample_usbd helper as the trackball) or Bluetooth (NCS HIDS), USB first. Battery level from VDDH, no divider.
    • Compiles for the DK and the Pro Micro, for all three concepts: 529 KB flash, 108 KB RAM for B.

    Printing

    Every part prints without supports. The largest is C's top shell at {{LARGEST}}, so any printer you're looking at, including 180 mm beds like the Bambu A1 mini, will do.

    1. Print the test coupon first

    stl/test_coupon.stl is a 15-minute strip with four switch holes from 13.9 to 14.2 mm (marked with 1 to 4 dots) and two insert holes. Use the hole where a switch clicks in firmly and doesn't rattle, then set sw_hole and insert_d in macropad.scad and rebuild. Printers differ by a tenth of a millimetre or two here, and this is the fit that matters most.

    2. Orientation and settings

    • Top shell: top surface down on the bed. The walls are square to the top surface, so they print straight up, and the insert holes face up.
    • Bottom plate and display clamp: flat.
    • Knob: top face down, for a smooth top.
    • PLA for test fits; PETG or PLA+ for the final case. 0.2 mm layers, 3–4 walls, 15–20% infill.

    3. Assembly order

    Inserts into the bosses, switches into the lid, PCB pressed onto the switches and screwed to the pillars, display and clamp, controller and wires, battery into the cradle, then the bottom plate and feet.

    4. Before the PCB

    You can print a case and hand-wire it first: switches in the lid, a diode on each, wires to the controller. It's a cheap way to check the feel and the screen position before ordering boards. dxf/pcb_outline_*.dxf has the board outline, MX centre holes and pillar holes for Altium.

    Chosen: B · Studio

    The 2.0″ screen is 320 × 240, which divides exactly into a 4 × 3 grid of 80-pixel tiles. Each key's icon and label then sits on the screen in the same place as the key itself, which is what makes a Stream Deck easy to use, without needing a screen in every key. It has the biggest screen, a full-size knob and 12 keys, and it still prints in one piece on any bed.

    The cost is width: the 2.0″ module's board is 58 mm long because the connector sits past the glass, which pushes the knob to the right and makes the case 101 mm wide. A suits a first, cheaper build. C suits you if you want more keys and mainly use the screen for the knob.

    Since the first version of this page: the firmware starter, the PCB inputs for Altium and a UK shopping list are done for B, with A and C kept working alongside. You chose B on 21 October. Next steps: buy the 2.0″ display module and an EC11 and measure them, and print the coupon. The screen and knob can run on the DK before any PCB exists.
